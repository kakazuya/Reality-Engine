### Transactions and replacement strategy

- Q3 call: the company bought one bulk carrier and one MR product tanker and sold one of each, plus a smaller bulk carrier and an older product tanker [Q3 call p.7].
- Q3 call: management said purchases were mainly replacements; growth investment will be slow [Q3 call p.7].
- Q3 call: management said older assets carry a significant premium, which is captured on sale and used to fund newer purchases [Q3 call p.7].
- Q4 call: management said older product tankers hitting 20 years in CY2024 are near end of economic life; the premium captured on their sale is recycled into younger ships [Q4 call p.13].
- Q4 call: management said at current prices it is cautious about going net long and is limiting capital put to work [Q4 call p.13].
- Q4 call: management said buying ships outright would need a very strong market for three to four years to justify the price [Q4 call p.13].
- Q4 call: management said it will explore switching crude and product tankers, with time before a decision [Q4 call p.15].
- Q4 call: management said the MR rate for the last two years averaged about USD 33,000-34,000 per day; a 20-year-old MR was worth about USD 10-12 million two years ago [Q4 call p.14].
- Q4 call: management said 20-year-plus ships, irrespective of condition, are not acceptable to some terminals and charterers because of date of build [Q4 call p.14-15].
- Q4 call: management said that in a hot market the oldest ship is the best ship to have, but customers and trades do not accept 20-year-plus ships [Q4 call p.15].
- Q4 call: management said it ran 25-year-old ships in the international market in 2004-2008, but that is not valid today [Q4 call p.14].
- Q3 call: management said MR tankers of about 18 years are trading worldwide because they are maintained to standard [Q3 call p.15].
- Q3 call: management said some customers will refuse ships beyond 20 years, but not at 16-17 [Q3 call p.15].
- Q3 call: management said in back-testing a 10-year-old ship earned a better return than a newer ship, because in strong markets the earnings gap is small [Q3 call p.16].
- Q3 call: example values given: a 17-year-old MR about USD 18-20 million vs a 12-year-old about USD 32 million [Q3 call p.15].
- Q3 call: management said a 10-year-old tanker is acceptable for at least eight years after purchase [Q3 call p.16].
- Q3 call: management said it bought five crude tankers in 2016-17; four were 5-7 years old and one was 17 years old [Q3 call p.16].
- Q3 call: management said it will not restrict itself to 10-year-old ships and will buy 6-7-year-old ships at a good price [Q3 call p.16].
- Q3 call: management said beyond 20 years a crude tanker becomes difficult to operate; flags allow crude tankers to run to 25 [Q3 call p.10].
- Q3 call: management said 20 years is the useful life used for crude tankers; a significant share of the crude fleet is older [Q3 call p.11].
- Q4 call: management said the LR2 product-tanker order book is largely Aframax-size LR2s that can move to the crude trade [Q4 call p.16].
- Q4 call: management said the option to trade as LR2 costs about USD 1.5-2 million on an Aframax that costs about USD 70 million to build (transcribed as "to bid") [Q4 call p.16].

### Scrapping and ageing

- Q3 call: management said ships are scrapped when operating them is too expensive or customers do not accept them, not for age alone; there is no statutory drop-dead date [Q3 call p.10].
- Q3 call: management said flag age limits exist, liberal for crude (25 years) [Q3 call p.10].
- Q3 call: management said scrapping would need earnings just above opex, as in calendar 2021, or a customer refusal to take old ships [Q3 call p.11].
- Q3 call: management said calendar 2021 was the weakest tanker market of the previous 30 years, and scrapping still stayed minimal [Q3 call p.11].
